Bomb Threat Preparedness & Response

A comprehensive guide for teachers and school heads — before, during, and after a bomb threat. This guide covers school personnel duties only. It stops where Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD) and law enforcement assume control.

⚠️

Before Using This App

Make sure your school has the following prepared:

  • School-Based Contingency Plan for Bomb Threat
  • Bomb Threat Incident Planning

These documents contain the whole details that the teaching staff will and should only know. They must be kept confidential to avoid leakage of the plan and safety protocols.

Critical reminders for school staff during bomb threats — plus common misconceptions corrected.

Know the pre-attack indicators

Unusual packages, unfamiliar persons loitering, repeated questions about schedules, or sudden changes in a student's behavior.

2

Evacuation routes, assembly areas, lockdown rooms, and the chain of command must be localized and posted in every classroom.

3

Lockdown, lockout, and evacuation drills at least twice per school year. Treat every drill as real.

4

Prepare your class go-bag

Class roster, emergency contacts, flashlight, whistle, first-aid kit, and a printed copy of this guide.

5

Establish reporting lines

Every teacher must know who to call first — the school head, the DRRM coordinator, and the local PNP station.

1

Stay calm and do not cut off the caller

If the threat is by phone, keep the caller talking. Note the exact words, background noise, voice, and time.

2

Do not touch or move any suspicious item

Do not use radios or mobile phones near it. Do not cover it. Do not place it in water.

3

Alert the school head / DRRM coordinator

Use the pre-arranged signal. Do not spread panic among students.

Headcount against your class roster. Report missing or unaccounted students immediately.

Handoff to EOD & Law Enforcement

Once EOD and the PNP arrive, surrender the area, provide your report, and follow their instructions. Do not re-enter the building until the official ALL CLEAR is given.

Goal Restore safety, account for everyone, and learn from the incident.
1

Wait for the official ALL CLEAR

Only EOD or the incident commander can declare the area safe. Never assume.

2

Provide Psychological First Aid (PFA)

Calm, comfort, and connect students to support. Watch for acute stress reactions.

3

Conduct headcount and medical triage

Re-account for all students and staff. Refer injuries to the school nurse or medical team.

4

Debrief and document

Record what happened, what worked, and what failed. Submit the incident report to the school head and Division DRRM coordinator.

5

Review and improve the plan

Update the contingency plan, retrain personnel, and schedule the next drill.

👥 Roles & Responsibilities

🧩 Every role has a purpose

In a bomb threat, confusion costs lives. Knowing exactly what each person is responsible for — and what they are not — keeps the response clean and prevents overlap.

🏫

School Head

Decision-making

Decides whether to lockdown, evacuate, or lockout. Holds final authority on all school-level decisions until EOD and law enforcement take over.

Authority coordination

Serves as the single point of contact for PNP, BFP, DRRMO, and other responding agencies. Escalates to EOD when needed.

Official communication

Issues all official statements to parents, the community, and the media. Approves any information released beyond the school gate.

📋

DRRM Coordinator

Emergency coordination

Activates the school contingency plan, coordinates staff assignments, and serves as the bridge between the school head and response units.

Information management

Receives and consolidates all reports from teachers and staff. Keeps the official incident log: what happened, when, where, and who reported it.

Evacuation / accountability monitoring

Tracks the status of each class and each evacuation site. Confirms that all groups have moved and been accounted for.

Documentation

Prepares the incident report after the response. Ensures all evidence, statements, and timestamps are properly recorded for authorities.

👩‍🏫

Teachers

Learner safety

Keep learners calm and secure. Never leave the class unattended during a threat. Use the magic word for orderly movement.

Evacuation

Lead the class along pre-planned routes to the designated evacuation area. Do not improvise or take shortcuts.

Headcount

Conduct a roll call by name — not just a number count — at the assembly point. Pair learners with buddies for faster checking.

Reporting missing / injured learners

Immediately report any unaccounted or injured learner to the DRRM coordinator. Do not send students to search.

🛡️

Security / Utility Personnel

Restrict access

Secure all gates. Prevent entry of unauthorized persons and prevent anyone from leaving with a student unless authorized.

Report suspicious observations

Report any suspicious persons, vehicles, or objects to the school head. Do not investigate or approach.

Assist with controlled movement

Guide traffic, keep corridors clear, and support evacuating classes without causing panic or congestion.

🎒

Learners

Follow instructions

Listen to the teacher, move quietly and in order, and stay with the class. Do not run, shout, or wander off.

Do not touch suspicious objects

Never pick up, open, kick, or cover any unattended bag, box, or item — even if it looks harmless.

Report suspicious items or threats

Tell a teacher or any school staff immediately if they see something strange or hear anyone talking about a bomb.

Key principle Everyone stays in their lane. The school head decides, the DRRM coordinator coordinates, the teachers protect learners, the security keeps the perimeter, and the learners follow. No one improvises a role they were not trained for.
🎭 Special Situations

🎯 Bomb threats don't always happen at a convenient time

Different school activities mean different crowds, different ages, and different risks. Each situation below needs a slightly adjusted response — but the core rules never change: do not touch, do not panic, evacuate calmly, and hand off to EOD.

📝
Bomb Threat During Examination

Stop the exam immediately. Do not allow students to bring answer sheets or test papers — evacuate as-is. Silent signal to proctors to avoid disruption. No student should be left in the testing room, and no paper should be collected mid-evacuation.

🇵🇭
Bomb Threat During Flag Ceremony

All learners are already assembled outdoors, which is an advantage. Teachers quickly form their classes and move to the alternate evacuation site — not the usual one, in case the ceremony area was the target. Maintain silence, dismiss by class, and avoid a stampede.

🎓
Bomb Threat During Graduation

Guests, parents, and VIPs are present — crowd control is the biggest risk. The school head or emcee calmly announces a short break or "photo opportunity" to avoid panic. Marshals guide guests to designated exits. Do not release students to parents until the official reunification point is set up.

🏀
Bomb Threat During Sports Events

Multiple schools or teams may be present. Coordinate with the event organizer and other coaches. Stop the game immediately. Clear the courts and bleachers in an orderly manner. Spectators exit first, athletes follow. Do not use the same exit for everyone.

👨‍👩‍👧
Bomb Threat During Parent Activities

Many unfamiliar adults are on campus. Adults must be guided firmly but calmly to the designated exit — they may want to retrieve their own children or belongings. Announce that students will be released through the official reunification process only. Parents should not block gates or rush to classrooms.

🍽️
Bomb Threat During Lunch / Recess

Students are scattered — canteen, hallways, comfort rooms, playground. This is one of the hardest situations to manage. Teachers must sweep their assigned zones quickly and gather students at class meeting points before moving. Canteen staff secure equipment and turn off gas. Do not let students run back for food or bags.

🌙
Bomb Threat Outside School Hours

Only guards, janitors, and possibly some teachers are on campus. The school head or duty officer decides whether to evacuate or stand by. Secure the area, deny entry, and wait for authorities. Do not enter the building to check anything alone. Notify the DRRM coordinator remotely.

⛈️
Threat During Severe Weather

Evacuating into a storm can be more dangerous than the threat itself. If there is heavy rain, strong wind, or flooding, consider lockdown in place until the weather clears — but keep students away from windows. The school head decides. Prioritize whichever risk is immediate.

🌋
Threat During Earthquake / Fire Response

If an earthquake or fire is already happening, that is the primary emergency — deal with it first. Do not attempt to carry out two evacuation plans at once. Once students are safe from the primary hazard, address the bomb threat with the same protocol: report, secure, wait for EOD.

🏕️
Threat When Learners Are Already Outside

This is the safest scenario — do not send learners back into the building. Keep them where they are, take a roll call by name, and move them further away if the threat zone is close. Hand off to EOD from the outdoor holding area. Re-entry only after the official ALL CLEAR.

Universal reminder No matter what activity is happening, the same core rules apply: do not touch suspicious items, keep learners calm, do not improvise evacuation routes, and stop where EOD and law enforcement take over.
📖 Terms & Definitions
BombAn explosive device designed to cause damage, injury, or death.
Bomb ThreatA communicated warning — verbal, written, or digital — that an explosive device will be detonated.
Bomb JokeA false statement about a bomb. It is a criminal offense in the Philippines, not a prank.
False AlarmA threat reported but no explosive device is found. Still requires full response and investigation.
BombingThe actual detonation of an explosive device.
EOD (Explosive Ordnance Disposal)The specialized unit trained to safely identify, disarm, and dispose of explosive devices. Once EOD arrives, the school's direct role ends and the scene is handed over to them.
DRRM (Disaster Risk Reduction and Management)The framework and coordinated efforts — planning, preparedness, response, and recovery — used by schools and government to reduce the impact of disasters and emergencies, including bomb threats.
IED (Improvised Explosive Device)A homemade bomb assembled from common materials and often disguised as everyday objects. Common components include mobile phone triggers, wires, batteries, and ammonium nitrate fuel oil (ANFO).

Provides a working model for tiered school response that can be adapted to other emergencies.

Balances accountability with child protection.

DepEd Order No. 40, s. 2012 DepEd Child Protection Policy The overarching child protection policy. Mandates zero tolerance for any act that endangers learners, including threats to their safety. Child protection

👁️ Suspicious Indicators — What to Look Out For

⚠️ Awareness, not alarm

These are not automatic signs of a bomb — but anything out of place, unexplained, or unusual for its surroundings should be reported to the school head. Do not investigate yourself. Do not touch.

📦 Objects & Items
🎒
Unattended bags, backpacks, or boxes

Any bag, box, or package left in a hallway, stairwell, restroom, or classroom that no one claims or recognizes.

📬
Parcel or item you did not order

A package or delivery arriving at the school or a teacher's desk that no one ordered, especially with no return address or a wrong name.

💉
Out-of-place item in a strange location

Example: a syringe box taped under a chair, a lunchbox inside a fire extinguisher cabinet, or a bottle of liquid in a trash can. Anything that doesn't belong where it is.

🛍️
Hanging plastic or bundle in a random spot

A plastic bag or bundle hanging from a ceiling, fence, tree, or gate — where no one would normally hang anything.

📻
Abandoned electronics

Radios, phones, laptops, or speakers left behind and still powered on or humming. Electronic devices can be triggers.

🕯️
Unusual smell or visible vapor

Chemical odor, burnt smell, or visible vapor near a container or area that should not have any. Do not approach — report from a distance.

🔌
Exposed wirings or unfamiliar circuits

Loose wires, wires taped to surfaces, or wiring that appears recently added and doesn't belong to the school's electrical system.

📦
Taped, sealed, or wrapped containers

Buckets, cans, or containers that are unusually taped, wrapped in plastic, or sealed with something other than the original lid.

🎁
Unexplained gift or package

A gift or box left at a classroom door or staff table without a note, name, or explanation.

⏱️
Audible ticking or beeping

A ticking, beeping, or humming sound coming from a bag, box, or area where nothing should be making noise.

🧍 Persons & Behavior
👀
Uneasy or unusual movements

A person pacing, sweating, fidgeting, avoiding eye contact, or behaving nervously around a specific area of the school.

🚶
Unfamiliar person loitering

Someone not in school uniform or without an ID, lingering near entrances, comfort rooms, or areas students do not normally gather.

📸
Photographing or filming exits and hallways

A person taking photos or videos of gates, exits, evacuation routes, or security checkpoints without a valid reason.

🗣️
Asking about schedules and security

Repeated or unusual questions about bell schedules, evacuation drills, security shifts, or which doors are unlocked.

🥷
Deliberate concealment

A person hiding their face, wearing unusually heavy clothing in warm weather, or carrying a large bag while avoiding contact.

😰
Extreme emotional distress

Someone visibly agitated, crying, or making cryptic remarks about "not being around" or "saying goodbye."

🚗 Vehicles
🅿️
Vehicle parked in an awkward location

A car, motorcycle, or tricycle parked unusually close to a building, in front of an exit, or in a spot not designated for parking.

🚚
Unfamiliar delivery vehicle

A delivery truck or van with no markings, no scheduled delivery, or a driver who refuses to identify themselves or the sender.

🚙
Occupied vehicle idling for a long time

A car with someone inside that has been parked and running for an unusually long period near the school gate or drop-off zone.

🚘
Vehicle with obscured plates or windows

License plates covered, tinted windows unusually dark, or a vehicle that appears modified in a suspicious way.

⚠️ Situations & Signs
🚪
Doors, windows, or locks tampered with

Doors forced open, windows broken, or locks showing signs of being picked or cut.

💬
Written or verbal threats

Graffiti, notes, chat messages, or spoken threats mentioning a bomb, explosion, or "something happening" at school.

🧪
Missing or moved lab chemicals

Chemicals, equipment, or supplies missing from the science lab, or found in places they should not be.

🔥
Small fires or burn marks

Small, unexplained fires, scorch marks, or burnt residue found in areas not meant for any fire activity.

📅
Threats tied to a specific date or event

Any rumor or message mentioning a specific date — exam day, graduation, foundation day — connected to a threat.

If you see anything suspicious Do not touch, move, cover, or photograph it. Do not use radios or phones near it. Clear the area, alert the school head, and call 911 / PNP.
📍 Vulnerable Areas — Risk Mapping

🎯 Know your school's weak points

Not every area of the campus is equally attractive to a would-be bomber. This section classifies common school zones into three risk levels so that staff can prioritize awareness and inspection — not conduct their own searches.

High RiskPrime targets
Medium RiskVulnerable spots
Low RiskOccasional checks
🔴 High Risk — Red9 areas
Science Laboratories & Storerooms

The primary target area for sourcing chemicals, glassware, and heating elements. Chemicals used in legitimate experiments can also be used to construct IEDs.

High Risk
Comfort Rooms & Restrooms

Secluded, rarely monitored, and regularly visited by students. Easy for someone to leave a device and walk away without being noticed.

High Risk
Storage Rooms & Janitorial Closets

Locked or poorly monitored spaces where suspicious items can be hidden for hours or days without being discovered.

High Risk
Main Entrance & Drop-off Zones

High-traffic areas with many unknown visitors. Ideal for someone to slip a bag, box, or package into the crowd during peak arrival and dismissal times.

High Risk
School Canteen & Kitchen Area

Dense with students during breaks and containing flammable materials such as LPG tanks, cooking oil, and cleaning chemicals. A blast here maximizes casualties.

High Risk
Assembly Grounds & Gymnasium

Where the whole school gathers. A device placed here — or at an evacuation site — can affect hundreds of students and staff at once.

High Risk
Electrical Rooms & Generator Sets

Contain transformers, breakers, and fuel. An explosion here can cause fire, power outage, and cascading damage to adjacent buildings.

High Risk
Motorpool / Vehicle Parking Area

Fuel tanks, school service vehicles, and unattended cars. A VBIED (vehicle-borne IED) can be driven or parked close to buildings.

High Risk
Unused / Vacant Classrooms

Rooms with no regular occupant are rarely inspected. Devices can be placed and left for extended periods.

High Risk
🟠 Medium Risk — Orange10 areas
Hallways & Corridors

Constant foot traffic during passing periods, but quiet between bells. Easy to leave an unattended bag against a wall or under a bench.

Medium Risk
Stairwells & Fire Exits

Essential for evacuation but also easy to block. Suspicious items left here can also interrupt escape routes during an actual incident.

Medium Risk
Library & Learning Resource Centers

Quiet, densely packed with paper and shelving. Students and staff may be slow to notice an unattended bag or parcel among the shelves.

Medium Risk
Computer Laboratories

Full of electronics, extension cords, and active wiring. A device hidden behind a CPU tower or under a desk can go unnoticed for hours.

Medium Risk
Faculty Room & Administrative Office

Central to school operations and often visited by outsiders. An incident here disrupts coordination and response.

Medium Risk
School Clinic / Health Room

Contains medicines, sharps, and sometimes oxygen tanks. Chemicals here can be misused, and a blast would eliminate first-aid capability during an emergency.

Medium Risk
Covered Courts & Open Stage

Large open spaces used for programs and P.E. Harder to search thoroughly due to size and elevated structures.

Medium Risk
School Gates & Perimeter Fences

First line of defense — and the easiest place for a device to be thrown over or handed off without anyone noticing.

Medium Risk
Garbage Bins & Waste Collection Points

Rarely checked inside. Common hiding spot for small IEDs or improvised devices disguised as trash.

Medium Risk
School-Based Feeding / Nutrition Areas

Contain cooking equipment, fuel, and large groups of young children during feeding programs.

Medium Risk
🟡 Low Risk — Yellow8 areas
Flag Pole & Ceremonial Grounds

Used during flag ceremonies only. Less visited throughout the day but still a symbolic target worth occasional inspection.

Low Risk
School Gardens & Landscaped Areas

Open, visible, and hard to hide a device in. Still worth a glance for disturbed soil or discarded items.

Low Risk
Waiting Sheds & Tricycle / Jeepney Stops

Transient areas used by parents and students. Devices may be left behind briefly, but people tend to notice forgotten belongings here.

Low Risk
Guidance Office & Counseling Rooms

Private, quiet spaces with limited traffic. Lower target value but worth awareness because of confidentiality.

Low Risk
School Supply & Stock Rooms (Non-Chemical)

Paper, uniforms, and general supplies. Flammable but not explosive — still, an unattended item here should be reported.

Low Risk
Bulletin Boards & Display Walls

Open and visible, but occasionally used to hide small items behind notices or posters.

Low Risk
Audio-Visual / Media Rooms

Contain electronics but are usually locked when not in use. Low foot traffic, but worth ensuring locked access.

Low Risk
Sports Equipment Storage

Balls, mats, and gear — mostly harmless. But the storage area may have poor lighting that could hide a small device.

Low Risk
How to use this section During a bomb threat, prioritize clearing and reporting areas by risk level — high first, then medium, then low. But remember: teachers do not search for devices. Clearing and reporting are the only actions within the school's scope. Actual search and disposal always belong to EOD and law enforcement.
🕵️ Who Are the Possible Bombers
Why this matters Knowing who typically makes bomb threats helps staff recognize patterns, respond appropriately, and avoid dangerous assumptions. This section is for awareness — not for profiling individuals.

💣 Terrorists / Organized Groups

Individuals or groups using bombs to advance an ideological, political, or religious agenda. Their threats are usually planned, coordinated, and may involve secondary devices. These are the most dangerous category and require the full response protocol.

🧠 Persons with Mental Health Problems

Individuals experiencing paranoia, delusions, or severe emotional distress. They may make threats without the actual capability to carry them out — but the threat must still be treated as real until proven otherwise.

🎭 Pranksters / Joke-makers

Mostly students or young people who think bomb jokes are funny. Even though they usually mean no harm, their actions are criminal under PD 1727 and trigger costly, disruptive responses. Still treated as real until cleared.

😠 Disgruntled Individuals / Revenge Seekers

Persons with a real or perceived grievance — former students, employees, or community members — who may use a threat to get back at the school or specific persons. Threats may be targeted and personal.

💰 Extortionists

Individuals or groups who use bomb threats to demand money or concessions. Common in transport, business, and sometimes institutional settings. The threat is used as leverage, but the device may still be real.

🎓 Students Avoiding an Exam or Event

A specific concern in schools: a student may call in a threat to cancel a test, delay a deadline, or escape a consequence. Regardless of the motive, the response must be the same — full protocol, no shortcuts.

🧪 Experimenters / Curious Individuals

Persons — often young — who mix chemicals or build small devices out of curiosity, without fully understanding the danger. Their "experiment" can still cause serious injury or death, and may be discovered accidentally by others.

Key point Motive does not change the response. Whether the caller is a terrorist, a prankster, or a student avoiding an exam — treat every bomb threat as real until EOD and law enforcement say otherwise.

🧠 Most Important Things to Remember

These are the critical reminders every school staff must internalize. In a bomb threat, seconds matter — these habits save lives.

🔍

Conduct a prior search at the evacuation area

Before sending students to any evacuation site, a designated staff member must visually sweep the area first.

Why? A secondary bomb may have been placed at the evacuation site to target people fleeing the first threat.

If anything suspicious is found, do not send students there. Redirect to the alternate site.

↔️

Establish a two-way evacuation area

Always prepare two evacuation sites in opposite directions — Site A and Site B.

If Site A is compromised, is blocked, or becomes unsafe, staff can immediately redirect to Site B without confusion or delay.

A single evacuation point is a single point of failure.

💥

If a bombing occurs — do not run

Running after a blast can lead you into the path of a secondary device or into falling debris.

Stay where you are if you are already in a cleared, protected area — away from windows, glass, and load-bearing structures.

If you are exposed, move calmly to the nearest cleared area. Do not rush in the open.

✨

Use a "magic word" to trigger a calm evacuation

Young children may panic if they sense danger. Instead, agree on a pre-planned magic word or phrase that tells staff to begin evacuation — without alarming the children.

The children should think it is simply a routine activity, a fire drill, or a game.

Example magic word
"Sunshine walk, everyone — line up quietly and follow me outside."

The magic word must be:

• Known only to school staff
• Short, natural, and non-threatening
• Practiced during drills so it feels routine
• Never explained to students in advance

✋

Do not touch, move, or cover anything suspicious

Never investigate an unattended item yourself. Never let a student check it. Never place it in water or cover it.

Clear the area and report to the school head and authorities immediately.

🤫

Keep calm and control information

Panic causes more injuries than the threat itself. Speak softly, give clear short instructions, and do not announce "there is a bomb" to students.

Only staff need to know the real reason for the evacuation.

✅

Wait for the official ALL CLEAR

Only EOD or the incident commander can declare the site safe. Do not re-enter any building based on rumors or assumptions.

Once cleared, provide Psychological First Aid to students and staff, then document and debrief.

🚫

Once evacuated — never go back inside

No matter how important the thing left behind is — a bag, a phone, a wallet, a laptop, a thesis, a car key — do not attempt to go back into the building.

A second device may be timed or triggered specifically to catch people who return. Life is more important than any material possession.

Report any missing item to the incident commander. Do not retrieve it yourself.

⚡

Some bombs are triggered by ordinary actions

IEDs may be detonated through a variety of triggers — not just a timer. Common triggers include:

• Motion — moving, lifting, or tilting the object
• Light — turning lights on or off, or opening a door
• Radio waves — mobile phones, two-way radios, or Wi-Fi signals
• Lasers — a beam of light interrupted by a person or object
• Vibrations — loud sounds, footsteps, or heavy vehicles

This is why you must not touch, move, cover, or use radios near a suspicious item.

🤝

Use silent and visual signals, not voices

If a threat is confirmed and students are nearby, do not shout instructions — shouting can trigger panic or, in some devices, vibration-based detonators.

Use hand signals, written notes, or pre-agreed gestures to communicate with other staff. Pre-arrange these signals during drills.

🎒

Leave belongings behind — they are not a priority

Students will instinctively want to grab their bags, phones, or projects. Train them to leave everything and move.

Bags can also conceal secondary devices, so picking them up is dangerous. The rule is simple: leave it, leave it now.

👷

Trust the professionals — do not second-guess EOD

Once EOD and law enforcement are on site, follow their instructions without argument — even if the instructions seem inconvenient or slow.

Do not volunteer to "check" anything. Do not try to help clear the building. Your job is now to support, not to act.

📋

Do a roll call by name, not by number

A headcount is not enough — call each student by name to confirm who is present and who is missing.

Students may hide, run to a different exit, or be pulled away by a parent. A name-by-name check catches these cases that a simple count will miss.

Report any unaccounted student to the incident commander immediately.

👥

Use a buddy system during evacuation

Pair students up — each student is responsible for their buddy. This reduces stragglers and helps teachers spot missing students faster.

Assign a buddy to students with special needs, younger children, or anyone who may need extra help moving.

📵

Stay off radios and mobile phones near the threat area

Radio frequency signals can trigger some IEDs. Do not use two-way radios, mobile phones, or Wi-Fi near a suspicious item.

Move to a safe distance before making calls. If possible, use landline or relay messages through a runner who is already outside the danger zone.

🪢

Watch for tripwires and disturbed surfaces

When moving through a cleared area, look for thin wires, fishing line, or disturbed ground — these may be tripwires or pressure triggers.

Do not step over, under, or around anything suspicious. Stop, back away slowly, and alert others.

🏫

Follow the official reunification procedure

Parents will rush to the school to pick up their children. Do not release students to anyone except through the official reunification point.

Verify identity, log every release, and only release students once the incident commander authorizes it. This prevents students from being taken by unauthorized persons during the chaos.

📝

Exhaust all means to document the threat — refer to CISA

Upon receiving the threat, gather every detail you can — exact words, time, voice, background noise, language used, and any identifying sounds. Use the CISA Bomb Threat Checklist as your guide.

The more information you capture in the first few minutes, the more useful it will be to investigators. Memory fades — write it down immediately.

✉️

If the threat came by letter — touch it as little as possible

A written threat is physical evidence. Every extra fingerprint on the paper makes the investigation harder.

Do not crumple it, do not fold it, do not write on it, do not place it in plastic. Pick it up by the edges only, place it in a clean paper envelope or folder, and hand it over to authorities as-is.

More fingerprints mean more hassle later — keep the letter in the condition it was found.

💾

If the threat came online — preserve it, do not delete it

Digital threats carry hidden data that can help investigators: metadata or EXIF inside images, the sender's email address, the IP address, timestamps, and platform account details.

Do not delete the message. Do not edit it. Do not forward it widely — forwarding can alter metadata. Take screenshots, note the platform (Facebook, SMS, email, etc.), and report to the school head and PNP immediately.

If it is an image file, keep the original file. Do not crop, resize, or re-upload it — those actions strip metadata.

Common Misconceptions

⚠️ Myth vs. Fact

These are the most common false beliefs about bomb threats and bombings. Correcting them is part of preparedness.

Myth

"A bomb threat is nothing serious — it's just a prank or a joke."

Fact

Every bomb threat must be treated as real until proven otherwise. In the Philippines, making a bomb joke is a criminal offense under PD 1727. Schools cannot afford to gamble on a guess.

Myth

"If I don't see a bomb, there is no bomb."

Fact

IEDs are designed to be hidden — inside bags, boxes, appliances, or everyday objects. Not seeing one does not mean it isn't there. Only EOD can confirm the area is clear.

Myth

"I should quickly check the suspicious bag myself before raising alarm."

Fact

Untrained handling of a suspicious item can trigger the device. Teachers must never touch, move, or open a suspicious object. Clear the area and report immediately.

Myth

"After the first explosion, it's safe to run out into the open."

Fact

Many bombing incidents use a secondary device timed to hit people fleeing the first blast. Do not run into the open. Stay in a cleared area or move calmly to one.

Myth

"One evacuation site is enough — we can all gather in the same place."

Fact

A single assembly point is a single point of failure. Schools should maintain two evacuation areas in opposite directions so one can be used if the other is compromised.

Myth

"The children need to know the truth so they cooperate."

Fact

Young children can panic and stampede, causing more injuries than the threat itself. Use a pre-agreed magic word to trigger a calm, orderly evacuation — the children do not need to know the real reason.

Myth

"Once the police arrive, teachers can relax and go back to class."

Fact

Teachers still have duties after handoff: accounting for students, Psychological First Aid, documentation, and debriefing. Re-entry is only allowed after the official ALL CLEAR from EOD.

Myth

"Posting about the incident online helps warn others."

Fact

Unverified posts can spread panic, compromise the investigation, and violate privacy. Do not post unverified information. Follow official communication channels only.

Myth

"A bomb threat is a good excuse to cancel class — the students will be fine."

Fact

The purpose of evacuation is safety, not convenience. Treating a threat as a class cancellation teaches students the wrong lesson and can cause panic or future false alarms. Handle it as a serious incident, not a holiday.

Myth

"Only big cities or high-profile schools get bomb threats."

Fact

Bomb threats have been reported in barangay schools, rural campuses, and small private institutions. No school is too small to be a target — and every school should have a plan.

Myth

"If it's a student prank, we don't need to call the police."

Fact

Even if the caller is later identified as a student, the threat is still a crime under PD 1727 and the school cannot verify it alone. Call the authorities — let them decide.

Myth

"We can just search the building ourselves and skip calling 911."

Fact

Teachers and school staff are not trained to search for or handle explosives. A self-conducted search can trigger the device or miss a hidden one. Call 911 and let EOD handle the search.

Myth

"Turning off the lights and hiding is always safer than evacuating."

Fact

Not always. A bomb can be triggered by light switches, motion, or a timer. The decision to lockdown or evacuate depends on the situation. Follow the contingency plan — do not improvise.

Myth

"A bomb always looks like a bomb — with wires, a clock, and red sticks."

Fact

Real IEDs rarely look like movie bombs. They are often hidden inside ordinary objects — a juice box, a book, a folder, a rice cooker, or a teddy bear. Anything unusual in the wrong place is suspicious.

Myth

"If a bomb-sniffing dog didn't alert, the area is safe."

Fact

Sniffer dogs are a tool, not a guarantee. Some explosives are sealed, masked, or formulated to evade detection. The official ALL CLEAR must come from EOD, not from a dog's reaction.

Myth

"The restroom, stairwell, or basement is a safe place to hide."

Fact

Small, enclosed, windowless spaces can concentrate blast pressure and debris. Follow your contingency plan for designated safe areas — do not hide in restrooms or stairwells unless trained and instructed.

Myth

"I can use my phone freely during an evacuation — it's just a phone."

Fact

Mobile phones emit radio frequency signals that can trigger some IEDs. Avoid using phones near a suspicious item. Also, network congestion during emergencies can block emergency calls — keep lines free for responders.

Myth

"Only teachers need to know the plan — students just follow."

Fact

Students, non-teaching staff, canteen workers, and security guards must also know the basics — where to go, what signal to follow, and what NOT to touch. A plan only staff know is a plan with holes.

Myth

"If you find a suspicious object, you should take it to the teacher or security office."

Fact

Do not carry or move it. Keep away from the object and immediately alert a teacher, school administrator, security personnel, or emergency responder.

Myth

"A bomb threat can safely be ignored if the caller sounds joking."

Fact

The apparent attitude of the caller does not determine whether the threat is safe. Information about the threat should be reported promptly to the proper authorities.

Myth

"Students should use their phones to record the incident."

Fact

Recording should not take priority over safety. Follow evacuation or shelter instructions and avoid interfering with emergency responders.

Remember In a bomb threat, the goal is not to be a hero — it is to keep every student safe and to hand over the situation to the professionals without adding to the danger.
